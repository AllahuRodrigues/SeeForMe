# CPSC 4900 Senior Project Proposal **Project Title:** **SeeForMeAR: Context-Aware Visual Assistance for Blind and Low-Vision Users** **Student:** Alláh-u-Abhá Rodrigues **Advisor:** Sohee Kim Park **Course:** CPSC 4900 Senior Project **Semester:** Fall 2026 ## 1. Introduction Computer vision models have become very good at recognizing what is present in an image. However, recognizing everything visible in a scene is not necessarily the same as communicating what is actually useful to a person. This distinction becomes especially important for assistive technology. Consider a blind or low-vision user walking through a hallway while pointing a phone camera forward. A vision model may simultaneously detect a chair, a backpack, a person, a door, a fire extinguisher, a poster, a trash can, and several pieces of text. A system that continuously reads every detection aloud may technically be accurate while still being difficult or even frustrating to use. Important information can become buried under repeated or irrelevant descriptions. My senior project, **SeeForMeAR**, will investigate how a real-time computer vision system can determine not only **what is visible**, but **what is worth communicating at that moment**. I will build a smartphone-oriented visual assistance prototype that detects objects, obstacles, and text from a live camera stream. On top of these perception models, I propose a context-aware cue-selection algorithm that assigns each possible spoken cue a priority based on factors including confidence, proximity, potential importance, novelty, text relevance, and whether the information has recently been communicated. The central research question is: **Can an uncertainty- and context-aware cue-selection algorithm reduce unnecessary spoken information while preserving or improving the communication of important visual information in real-time assistive computer vision?** The project therefore combines computer vision, machine learning, algorithms, mobile software engineering, human-computer interaction, and accessibility. My goal is to produce both a working interactive system and a quantitative research evaluation rather than simply combining existing APIs into an application. --- ## 2. Background and Related Work There is significant prior work using computer vision to assist blind and low-vision users. Microsoft Seeing AI, for example, uses smartphone cameras and artificial intelligence to recognize text, objects, products, people, currency, and scenes and communicate this information through audio. This demonstrates that modern computer vision can provide useful visual information through a mobile device. Academic work, however, also demonstrates why visual assistance remains challenging in real-world settings. Gurari et al. introduced **VizWiz**, a dataset containing more than 30,000 visual questions originating from blind users. Unlike standard computer-vision datasets, VizWiz images frequently contain poor framing, blur, difficult lighting, and other characteristics produced in genuine assistive use. Their experiments showed that images produced in this setting remain difficult for computer-vision models. Chiu et al. later investigated image-quality problems in images captured by blind users and showed that issues such as blur, poor lighting, and framing can make scenes difficult or impossible for automated systems to interpret. Bafghi and Gurari further demonstrated that image classifiers trained on conventional datasets experience substantial difficulty when evaluated on photographs captured by blind users. This suggests that strong performance on benchmark datasets does not automatically translate to strong performance in an accessibility context. Text is particularly important. Singh et al. found that reading text represents a major category of visual questions asked by visually impaired users and developed the TextVQA benchmark for models that must jointly recognize visual content and text. Other research has explored more specialized real-time systems. For example, LineChaser uses smartphone RGB and depth sensing to help blind users identify and follow a line of people, communicating navigation information using audio and vibration. These systems establish that visual perception can provide meaningful accessibility assistance. My project focuses on a different question: **once the system has generated many possible observations, how should it choose which observations to communicate?** The proposed contribution is therefore not a new object detector or OCR model. Instead, SeeForMeAR will introduce and experimentally evaluate a **context-aware prioritization layer between visual perception and user feedback**. --- ## 3. Problem Description A straightforward assistive vision pipeline can be represented as: **Camera → Object/Text Detection → Text-to-Speech** The weakness of this pipeline is that every sufficiently confident prediction may become a spoken message. Suppose that across several consecutive frames a model detects: * PERSON, confidence 0.97 * CHAIR, confidence 0.94 * DOOR, confidence 0.92 * EXIT sign, confidence 0.89 * BACKPACK, confidence 0.84 If the system simply speaks detections in descending confidence order, the user might hear: > “Person. Chair. Door. Exit. Backpack.” The same objects may then be detected again fractions of a second later. This produces three problems. First, **model confidence is not equivalent to user importance**. A chair directly in a walking path may be more important than a person far away even if the person receives a larger detection confidence. Second, continuous recognition creates **temporal redundancy** because objects persist across many consecutive video frames. Third, spoken interaction has limited bandwidth. Unlike a visual bounding-box interface, several audio messages cannot be consumed simultaneously. The project therefore treats feedback generation as a ranking and filtering problem. For every candidate observation \(i\), the system will compute a cue priority: $$ S_i = \alpha C_i + \beta R_i + \gamma N_i + \delta P_i + \eta T_i - \lambda D_i $$ where: * \(C_i\) = model confidence, * \(R_i\) = estimated contextual relevance, * \(N_i\) = novelty of the observation, * \(P_i\) = proximity or potential navigation importance, * \(T_i\) = text/sign importance, * \(D_i\) = recent-duplicate penalty. The highest-priority observations exceeding a decision threshold will be communicated. Low-confidence, repeated, or low-value detections can remain silent. I will refer to this method as the **Context-Aware Cue Selection algorithm (CACS)**. The exact scoring function may evolve during experimentation. Part of the project will investigate whether learned weighting or manually defined weighting gives better results. --- ## 4. Planned Approach ### 4.1 System Architecture The planned system is: **Live Smartphone Camera** ↓ **Frame Preprocessing** ↓ **Object Detection + OCR** ↓ **Tracking / Temporal Memory** ↓ **Candidate Visual Events** ↓ ### **CACS Prioritization Algorithm** Confidence * relevance * proximity * novelty * text importance − repetition ↓ **Selected Cue** ↓ **Audio / Optional Haptic Feedback** A small visual debugging interface will simultaneously display bounding boxes, confidence scores, and the cue selected by CACS. This will make the internal decisions of the algorithm visible during development and will also create a strong interactive demonstration for the final poster session. ### 4.2 Visual Perception I plan to evaluate lightweight computer-vision models suitable for real-time inference rather than training a very large model from scratch. Candidate tools include: * YOLO-family lightweight object detectors; * MobileNet-based detection models; * OpenCV for camera processing and tracking; * EasyOCR, PaddleOCR, or platform-native OCR; * PyTorch during model experimentation; * ONNX, TensorFlow Lite, Core ML, or a similar mobile inference framework for deployment. The final model choice will depend on the accuracy/latency tradeoff measured during early experiments. ### 4.3 Temporal Object Tracking Frame-by-frame object detection alone would repeatedly rediscover the same objects. The prototype will therefore associate detections across neighboring frames using lightweight object tracking. Each tracked object will maintain a small state containing information such as: $$ (\text{class},\text{confidence},\text{location},\text{age},\text{last announced}) $$ This temporal memory allows CACS to determine whether an object is new, whether its location has changed significantly, and whether the system recently described it. ### 4.4 Context-Aware Cue Selection CACS will rank candidate cues at each decision step. For example: **Observation A:** chair directly ahead, 0.83 confidence, never announced **Observation B:** poster on wall, 0.95 confidence, announced two seconds ago A confidence-only system chooses the poster. CACS should choose the chair. Similarly, text classified as potentially important—for example **EXIT**, **STOP**, or room-number signage—can receive greater priority than arbitrary decorative text. An uncertainty threshold will also prevent low-confidence predictions from immediately becoming authoritative spoken statements. ### 4.5 Baselines I will compare CACS against at least two simpler baselines: **Baseline 1 — Confidence Threshold** Speak every object whose detection confidence exceeds a fixed value. **Baseline 2 — Confidence + Cooldown** Speak confident detections but suppress identical object labels for a fixed period. **Proposed Method — CACS** Rank candidate observations using confidence, context, novelty, temporal history, and relevance. This comparison is important because it transforms the application into a falsifiable experiment. --- ## 5. Evaluation The project will measure both the visual perception system and the cue-selection algorithm. ### Detection Performance Where labeled data are available, I will measure metrics such as precision, recall, and detection performance for objects and OCR accuracy for text. I will also evaluate the system on challenging examples similar to real assistive camera use, including: * motion blur; * unusual camera angles; * partial objects; * low lighting; * cluttered environments; * text occupying a small portion of the image. ### Real-Time Performance For interactive use, latency is critical. I will measure: * inference latency per frame; * end-to-end cue latency; * frames processed per second; * memory usage where practical. The target is interactive performance on consumer hardware, with latency low enough that spoken observations remain relevant to the current scene. ### Cue-Selection Performance The most important experiment will evaluate whether CACS communicates information more efficiently than the baselines. For a collection of controlled video scenarios, I will construct or annotate a set of events that should reasonably be considered important. Metrics will include: **Important-cue recall** $$ \frac{\text{important events announced}} {\text{total important events}} $$ **Cue precision** $$ \frac{\text{useful cues announced}} {\text{all cues announced}} $$ **Audio load** ** audio level** Number of spoken cues per minute. **Duplicate rate** Fraction of spoken messages that unnecessarily repeat recently provided information. **Critical-event delay** Time between the appearance of an important object or sign and the system communicating it. The ideal system should preserve high important-event recall while substantially reducing audio load and duplicate messages. ### Ablation Study I will also remove individual parts of CACS to test which signals actually matter. For example: * CACS without novelty; * CACS without proximity; * CACS without uncertainty; * CACS without temporal memory. This analysis will help determine whether improvements come from the complete method or from only one component. --- ## 6. Ethical and Accessibility Considerations Assistive computer vision has a particularly important failure mode: an incorrect prediction may be communicated to someone who cannot independently verify the visual information. For this reason, SeeForMeAR will be presented as an experimental assistive-information system rather than a replacement for mobility tools such as a cane or guide dog. The system will also explicitly consider uncertainty. Predictions below an appropriate confidence threshold will either be suppressed or described cautiously rather than presented as certain. Privacy is another consideration because a continuously operating camera may capture people, documents, addresses, or other sensitive information. Wherever technically practical, visual processing will be performed locally and frames will not be permanently stored by the application. A full study involving blind or low-vision participants would require appropriate human-subjects planning and is outside the guaranteed deliverables for this one-semester project. The primary thesis evaluation will therefore be based on reproducible benchmark and controlled-scene experiments. --- ## 9. Future Work Several extensions would be possible beyond the Fall 2026 semester. The cue-selection policy could eventually be personalized based on what individual users consider important. A learned model could replace the manually structured scoring function and learn preferences from interaction history. The system could also incorporate depth estimation, spatial audio, haptic feedback, visual-language models, indoor localization, and wearable cameras. The most important future step would be a formal accessibility study involving blind and low-vision participants to determine whether improvements in the quantitative cue-selection metrics correspond to meaningful improvements in real use. --- ## 10.
